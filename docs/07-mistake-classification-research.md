# Severity, difficulty and cause: three separate jobs

Chess mistake frameworks fall into three families that answer different questions. **Engine schemes measure severity**: how much a move cut the mover's winning chances. **Human-centred models estimate difficulty**: how likely a player at a given level was to find the right move or to fall into the wrong one. **Coaches' taxonomies name causes**: why the player went wrong. No published framework does all three, and no study has validated any of them for players rated under 1000. For chesster, the most urgent finding is a factual error in the repo. Lichess's thresholds are 0.1 / 0.2 / 0.3 on a [-1, +1] "winning chances" scale, which is **5 / 10 / 15 Win% points**, not 10 / 20 / 30 ([lila Advice.scala](https://github.com/lichess-org/lila/blob/master/modules/tree/src/main/Advice.scala)). chesster's 20-point "mistake" is already past Lichess's blunder line, and its 30-point "blunder" is twice Lichess's. The thresholds themselves are defensible for a beginner who wants only result-changing swings, but they are Chess.com-sized, not Lichess-sized, and should be labelled that way. The research also explains the depth-4 experiment. At around 800, almost every blunder is refuted shallowly, so search depth cannot tell hard mistakes from easy ones. Count-of-safe-moves and Maia probabilities are the better-supported difficulty signals. Cause should stay a human judgement, with the tool offering computed hints only after the user's own review. One profile note: the user's Chess.com rapid rating is now **773–816**, not the ~700 the docs assume, so the docs' Lichess-equivalent band (1000–1200) needs checking too. No primary source for a Chess.com-to-Lichess conversion was found.

## Lichess's "blunder" is 15 Win% points, half of chesster's

**Lichess** converts Stockfish centipawns to winning chances with `wc = 2/(1+exp(-0.00368208·cp)) − 1`, clamped to [-1, +1]. Win% is `50 + 50·wc`, with cp capped at ±1000 ([scalachess eval.scala](https://github.com/lichess-org/scalachess/blob/master/core/src/main/scala/eval.scala)). A move is judged by the change in the mover's wc from before to after the move. The judgement list is `.3 → Blunder, .2 → Mistake, .1 → Inaccuracy` ([lila Advice.scala](https://github.com/lichess-org/lila/blob/master/modules/tree/src/main/Advice.scala)). The browser's live annotations use the same raw-wc cut-offs ([liveAnnotate.ts](https://github.com/lichess-org/lila/blob/master/ui/analyse/src/liveAnnotate.ts)). Because one wc unit equals 50 Win% points, the thresholds are **5, 10 and 15 Win% points**. The coordinator checked this independently against the same source files. The "30% = blunder" reading circulates on Lichess's own forum and contradicts the code ([Lichess forum](https://lichess.org/forum/general-chess-discussion/what-constitutes-an-inaccuracy)). This is probably how the repo's "Lichess 10/20/30" label arose. Mate transitions have their own rule. Walking into a forced mate is a Blunder unless the prior eval was already below −700 cp (then Mistake) or −999 cp (then Inaccuracy). Losing a forced mate is graded the same way from the resulting eval. A slower mate is never flagged ([Advice.scala](https://github.com/lichess-org/lila/blob/master/modules/tree/src/main/Advice.scala)). The curve constant was refit in July 2022 on 75,000 positions from **2300+ rated rapid games**. The author noted it "should move toward −0.002 as ELO decreases" ([lila PR #11148](https://github.com/lichess-org/lila/pull/11148)). Lichess Accuracy % is a fitted exponential of the Win% drop. Game accuracy blends a volatility-weighted mean with a harmonic mean ([AccuracyPercent.scala](https://github.com/lichess-org/lila/blob/master/modules/analyse/src/main/AccuracyPercent.scala)). chesster hides accuracy anyway.

**Chess.com** grades by Expected Points lost, on a 0–1 scale estimated from **the player's rating plus the engine eval**. The bands are Best 0, Excellent ≤0.02, Good ≤0.05, Inaccuracy ≤0.10, Mistake ≤0.20, Blunder >0.20. The eval needed to count as "winning" also varies by rating ([Chess.com Help](https://support.chess.com/en/articles/8572705-how-are-moves-classified-what-is-a-blunder-or-brilliant-and-etc)). Since Game Review v2, a blunder must also lose material or allow mate. A new "Miss" label covers failing to punish an opponent's error ([Chess.com News](https://www.chess.com/news/view/chesscom-launches-game-review-v2)). The EP curve, the Miss threshold and the CAPS2 accuracy formula are unpublished ([Chess.com Help: Accuracy](https://support.chess.com/en/articles/8708970-how-is-accuracy-in-analysis-determined)). EP and Win% are only roughly comparable, because EP includes draws and is rating-conditioned. Even so, a 0.20 EP blunder is close to chesster's 20-point mistake. Among open-source tools, **En Croissant** ports the Lichess formula with 5 / 10 / 20 Win% cut-offs ([score.ts](https://github.com/franciscoBSalgueiro/en-croissant/blob/master/src/utils/score.ts)). **Lucas Chess** uses its own curve and compares the best move with the played move, with defaults of 3.3 / 7.5 / 15.5 ([Configuration.py](https://github.com/lukasmonk/lucaschessR2/blob/main/bin/Code/Config/Configuration.py)). **Fritz** has a single user-set cp threshold: 60 by default, with 300 meaning "only grave blunders" ([Fritz 18 Help](https://help.chessbase.com/Fritz/18/Eng/000019.htm)). The original fixed **50 / 100 / 300 cp** scheme was Lichess's before it switched to winning chances ([TalkChess](https://talkchess.com/forum3/viewtopic.php?p=682307)). Its flaw is that a 200 cp loss reads the same from 0.0 → −2 as from +7 → +5 ([Lichess forum](https://lichess.org/forum/lichess-feedback/perfect-playing-according-to-lichess-but-one-player-lost-the-game)).

| Scheme | Scale | Inaccuracy | Mistake | Blunder | Rating-aware? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lichess (current) | Win% drop, before vs after move | 5 | 10 | 15 | No (curve fitted on 2300+) |
| En Croissant | Win% drop (Lichess curve) | 5 | 10 | 20 | No |
| Lucas Chess | own 0–100 curve, best vs played | 3.3 | 7.5 | 15.5 | No |
| Chess.com | Expected Points lost (×100) | 5–10 | 10–20 | >20 + material/mate | Yes, unpublished curve |
| Old Lichess / folklore | centipawns | 50 | 100 | 300 | No |
| **chesster draft** | Win% drop, Lichess curve | — | **20** | **30** | No |

Using the Lichess curve, a 20-point drop from equality is about a **230 cp** swing and a 30-point drop about **376 cp**. Lichess's 15-point blunder is about 168 cp (computed from the formula). The user's non-negotiable is to surface only swings of about 2+ points that changed the likely result. A 20-point bar matches that rule well. Lichess's blunder line would also surface many sub-2-pawn swings. The weakness common to every scheme in the table is that the curve assumes strong players. Duolingo's engineers make the point concretely: a GM up a bishop almost always wins, while a beginner in the same spot is "close to even". They therefore shift the sigmoid by user level ([Duolingo Blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/engineering-game-review/)). Chess.com does the same through EP. The open-source tools do not.

## Difficulty predicts human blunders better than rating or clock does

The strongest academic result for a review tool is **Anderson, Kleinberg & Mullainathan (KDD 2016)**. They used tablebase endgames as perfect ground truth and defined blunder potential **β** as the share of legal moves that worsen the game-theoretic result. β alone predicted whether a human blundered with **0.73** accuracy on balanced data. Skill alone reached 0.55, and time alone 0.53. For FICS players rated 1200–1800, a 0.2 rise in β outweighed a 600-point rating gap. Clock mattered sharply only in the last ~10 seconds of 3-minute games ([arXiv 1606.04956](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1606.04956)). Long thinks were also associated with more blunders, plausibly as a sign of uncertainty. The limit is that these were 6-piece endgames. Extending the result to middlegames is plausible but unproven.

**Regan's** work adds two findings. First, raw centipawn loss grows with the absolute evaluation, even close to equality, and is larger for the side that is behind. Measuring loss as ln(1+|x|) levels this out, and makes a 2-pawn and a 5-pawn blunder "equally fatal" ([Regan & Haworth 2011](https://www.cse.buffalo.edu//~regan/papers/pdf/ReHa11c.pdf)). Win% transforms do the same job. Second, **"swing"** measures how much a move's value changes with search depth. For 2700-rated players, the rate of finding the best move falls from about 70% to 30% as swing-up rises from the 0–1 bucket to the 4–5 bucket. The **depth of satisficing**, the depth at which a played move stops looking best, rises with rating from near zero for novices ([Regan, Gödel's Lost Letter](https://rjlipton.com/2015/10/06/depth-of-satisficing/); [Biswas & Regan 2015](https://www.scitepress.org/papers/2015/52883/52883.pdf)). That last point predicts chesster's experiment. If novices satisfice at roughly depth zero, their losing moves are refuted at depth one or two. A depth-4 search then sees all of them, as happened with all 31 flags from 16 October. Shallow depth is not a broken signal. It is simply saturated at this rating. The Ljubljana group (Guid & Bratko; Stoiljkovikj, Bratko & Guid) reached a similar conclusion from another direction: raw engine output is a poor proxy for human difficulty, so they model human-like "meaningful search trees" instead ([Guid & Bratko via ChessBase](https://en.chessbase.com/post/using-che-engines-to-estimate-human-skill); [COBISS](https://plus.cobiss.net/cobiss/si/en/bib/1536704963)).

The cognitive literature supports a short list of causes. Less thinking time raised GM blunders from **5.02 to 6.85 per 1,000 moves** (rapid versus classical), with blunders defined as ≥1.5 pawns ([Gobet's reply restating Chabris & Hearst](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/1391/1/Chabris_Hearst_Reply.pdf)). The **Einstellung** effect, where a familiar first idea blocks a better one and steers the eyes, has experimental and eye-tracking support. When the familiar move was an outright blunder, though, all experts and most novices avoided it ([Sheridan & Reingold 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3790829/)). Gobet adds two warnings. Engine detection is "a rather blunt way of measuring errors" that catches only gross tactical mistakes. Blunders are not independent: one raises the chance of another ([Gobet reply](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/1391/1/Chabris_Hearst_Reply.pdf)). No study found builds a validated taxonomy of blunder causes such as hung piece versus missed threat versus miscalculation. No study found classifies the causes of sub-1200 blunders either.

## Maia measures human likelihood, but only coarsely

**Maia-1** (KDD 2020) treated "will a human blunder here?" as its own task, defining a blunder as a drop of ≥10 points of win probability. A residual CNN reached **67.7%** balanced accuracy from the board alone and 71.7% with ratings, clock and eval added. The move-prediction models did far worse at this than the specialised blunder models ([arXiv 2006.01855](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.01855v3)). **Maia-2** is a single network conditioned on rating bins from (0,1000] upward and trained on Lichess **rapid** only. Its predictions change monotonically with rating in **~27%** of positions, against ~1% for Maia-1. In a mate-in-1 case study, its probability of the correct move rose smoothly from 0.22 to 0.45 across ratings ([arXiv 2409.20553](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.20553v2)). **Maia-3 / Chessformer** reaches 57.1% move matching but is trained on Lichess **blitz**. Its accuracy plateaus around 50% at low ratings, which the authors attribute to high randomness in low-level play ([arXiv 2605.19091](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.19091v1)). Its UCI release outputs a predicted outcome for human games, not an engine eval ([CSSLab/maia3](https://github.com/CSSLab/maia3)). maiachess.com's Blunder Meter and Moves-by-Rating panels are built on Maia-2. The site does not publish how they combine Maia with Stockfish or what thresholds they use ([Maia Platform v1](https://www.maiachess.com/blog/platform-v1)). Every Maia generation drops moves made with under 30 seconds on the clock, so clock-driven lapses fall outside its training data.

The closest prior attempt at chesster's crossover idea failed. Chess Engine Lab tried to rate a mistake by "the strongest Maia that still played the wrong move". With Maia-1's separate per-rating models, "in most positions the Maia models agreed" ([Chess Engine Lab](https://chessenginelab.substack.com/p/testing-maias-puzzle-performance)). Maia-2's single rating-conditioned model should behave more smoothly, but 27% monotonicity means many positions will have no crossover or several. Puzzle-difficulty research sets the precision ceiling. The FedCSIS 2025 winners combined fine-tuned Maia-2 embeddings with gradient boosting and reached an MSE of 52.3k, about **±229 rating points**. Errors were worst below 1000 and above 2400 ([FedCSIS 2025 overview](https://annals-csis.org/Volume_43/drp/pdf/5937.pdf)). The user's band sits at that weak low end. Personalised models (Maia4All) need only ~20 games but add under two points of top-1 accuracy ([arXiv 2507.21488](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.21488v1)). That gain is too small to justify the complexity in a personal tool.

## Coaches classify the process failure, not the move

**Dan Heisman** offers the most usable cause model for this level. Its central idea is **Hope Chess**: "making moves without analyzing the opponent's possible forcing replies". *Basic* Hope Chess (no safety check at all) belongs to advanced beginners. *Passive* Hope Chess (glancing for familiar patterns without analysing) belongs to players rated 1200–1700 ([Heisman, Chess.com](https://www.chess.com/article/view/passive-vs-basic-hope-chess)). He lists 18 thought-process errors, separate from "thought content errors". They include playing too fast or too slow for the clock situation, assuming one reply, not recognising a critical position, and Kotov syndrome ([Heisman blog](https://www.chess.com/blog/danheisman/types-of-chess-thought-process-errors)). According to a secondhand review, he also grades piece safety in five levels: en prise, counting, single tactical motif, combination, sacrificial combination ([Forward Chess review](https://forwardchess.com/blog/book-review-a-guide-to-chess-improvement/)). **Noël Studer** files each loss under its *last decisive* mistake ("Only sign the mistake that really leads to the loss"), in one of four buckets: Opening Theory, Tactics/Calculation, Strategical play, Endgame. He recommends using at least the last ten losses ([Studer](https://nextlevelchess.com/most-effective-chess-training/)). He splits cause into chess understanding versus decision process. He also insists that the origin of a mistake comes from the player's own thoughts at the board, written down before the engine is opened ([Studer](https://nextlevelchess.com/game-analysis-mistake/); [Studer Part 2](https://nextlevelchess.com/game-analysis-2/)).

The classic authors are mostly diagnostic rather than taxonomic. **Kotov** gave candidate moves, the analysis tree, and the syndrome of a long fruitless think followed by a hasty blunder ([Chess.com glossary](https://chess.com/terms/kotov-syndrome-chess)). **Krogius** described the *retained image*, calculating with a piece that has already moved, and the *inert image*, carrying a stale evaluation forward. This comes from a secondhand review ([BPS The Psychologist](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/errare-humanum-est)). **Rowson**'s seven sins (Thinking, Blinking, Wanting, Materialism, Egoism, Perfectionism, Looseness) are metacognitive attitudes ([Silman review](https://www.jeremysilman.com/book-review/seven-deadly-chess-sins/)). Of these, "Blinking", missing the critical moment, is the one most usable at 800. Explicit error taxonomies from Dvoretsky, Aagaard, Silman and others could not be verified and are not attributed here. Taken together, the coaching sources share five axes: phase/skill, process versus content, tactical depth, clock, and psychological state. chesster's H/M/T/K/C/O/E tags cover phase/skill, part of tactical depth, and clock. They do not cover **process versus content**, the axis Heisman and Studer both treat as central. Neither the coaching nor the academic sources offer a validated cause taxonomy to borrow. The user's seven tags are a reasonable personal convention, not a deviation from a standard.

## What this means for chesster

This section separates what the sources establish from what is inferred for chesster's design. Everything under "inference" is a design proposal that has not been validated on sub-1000 players.

**Evidence.** Lichess thresholds are 5 / 10 / 15 Win% points, calculated before versus after the move on a curve fitted to 2300+ players ([Advice.scala](https://github.com/lichess-org/lila/blob/master/modules/tree/src/main/Advice.scala); [PR #11148](https://github.com/lichess-org/lila/pull/11148)). Chess.com's Blunder band is >0.20 EP and requires material loss or allowed mate ([Chess.com News](https://www.chess.com/news/view/chesscom-launches-game-review-v2)). Position difficulty (β) predicts blunders far better than rating or clock ([Anderson et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1606.04956)). Novice depth of satisficing is near zero ([Regan](https://rjlipton.com/2015/10/06/depth-of-satisficing/)). Maia-2 is rapid-trained and only partly monotonic across ratings ([arXiv 2409.20553](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.20553v2)). Difficulty estimates carry roughly ±230-point error, worst at low ratings ([FedCSIS 2025](https://annals-csis.org/Volume_43/drp/pdf/5937.pdf)). Coaches locate the cause in the player's own thought process ([Studer](https://nextlevelchess.com/game-analysis-2/); [Heisman](https://www.chess.com/article/view/passive-vs-basic-hope-chess)).

**Inference: severity rule.** Keep the 20 / 30 Win% bars, but rename them in the repo as "about 2× Lichess, close to Chess.com's EP bands". Drop the "Lichess thresholds" description, which is wrong. Copy the Lichess mate rules (the −700 / −999 cp grading, never flag a delayed mate) and the ±1000 cp clamp, so labels match a known reference. Pin one depth or node count for every position, so labels are reproducible. A flatter curve is a possible later option. With k = −0.002, as the Lichess PR author suggested for low Elo, a 20-point drop from equality needs about 424 cp instead of 230 (computed). It would better reflect how undecided a +3 position is at 800, but no one has validated the constant at this level. Treat it as an experiment, not a default.

**Inference: difficulty signals.** Drop depth-based difficulty. The 16 October experiment and Regan's novice-satisficing finding agree that it saturates. Add a **safe-move count** from Stockfish MultiPV: how many legal moves stay within the threshold. Its two extremes are "only move: 1 of 34 holds" and "30 of 34 were fine and you chose a losing one". This is the cheapest signal with the strongest published support, and the "easy" end marks the best lesson candidates. Run Maia-2 at fixed ratings, with the opponent's rating fixed too, since it shifts predictions in up to 6% of positions. Use it to report P(best move) and P(played move). A high P(played move) marks a "natural, tempting" mistake. Build the N/L/X/R crossover from these probabilities. Report it in coarse bands with a minimum probability gap. Give explicit outcomes for "no crossover" and "multiple crossovers" rather than forcing them into N or L. Prefer Maia-2 (rapid) over Maia-3 (blitz) for a 15+10 player. Re-derive the user's Lichess-rapid equivalent from the current 773–816 Chess.com rating instead of the documented ~700.

**Inference: cause hints, shown only after the user's own review.** Several of the user's tags can be suggested from PGN plus engine data. **H**: material lost on the next ply, graded by Heisman's safety level (en prise, counting, motif). **C**: the `%clk` value at the swing, or a move much faster or slower than the user's median. With a 10-second increment, true clock collapse should be rare, so require clock evidence before suggesting C. **O**: the swing falls within the first ~10–12 moves. **E**: a material-count endgame where a winning eval was not converted. **K**: mate appears in the refutation line. **M vs T**: M if the refutation exploits a threat the opponent had just created; T if the missed best move was the user's own tactic. Chess.com's Miss covers the T-type case. Present these as "the engine suggests H" beside the user's tag, never in place of it. That respects the repo's order of hot notes, then own review, then engine check, then one lesson.

**What stays manual.** The *why* stays manual: whether the user skipped the safety check, glanced, or analysed and miscalculated. This is Heisman's Basic versus Passive Hope Chess and Studer's process-versus-understanding split. No engine can recover it, and it lives in the paper notebook. Adding a one-letter process mark to H/M/K tags would capture the axis the current tags miss, at almost no cost. Choosing the single lesson also stays manual. One aggregate rule is worth borrowing from Studer: for monthly tallies, count only the last decisive mistake per loss.

## Conclusion

Most of chesster's design survives this review. One correction is needed: the 20/30 bars are fine, and the "Lichess" label is not. The bigger change is in where difficulty should come from. The evidence points away from engine depth and toward the shape of the position (how many moves were safe) and toward human-likelihood models. Those models are currently precise only to a few hundred rating points, and least precise exactly at the user's level. The N/L/X/R crossover therefore works best as a coarse, honest flag that can say "unclear", not as a rating.

The deeper takeaway is that severity, difficulty and cause are independent axes, and the most useful lesson sits where they meet: a large swing in an easy position, with a cause the user can name from memory. The tool can compute the first two axes well. The third is where improvement actually happens, which is why it belongs to the player and the notebook.
